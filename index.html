Marie Curie

The scientist who lit the way for radioactivity — and for women in science

Her Story

Long before she became one of the most celebrated scientists in history, Maria Skłodowska was a girl growing up in Russian-controlled Warsaw, at a time when women were barred from higher education in her homeland. Determined to learn anyway, she studied in secret at an underground institution known as the Flying University, and worked for years as a governess to save enough money to eventually join her sister in Paris. She arrived at the Sorbonne in the early 1890s with almost nothing, living in an unheated attic room and surviving on little more than bread and tea, driven entirely by her hunger to study physics and mathematics.

It was in Paris that she met Pierre Curie, a physicist whose passion for research matched her own. Their partnership became one of science's great collaborations: together they investigated a strange, invisible energy given off by uranium ore, a phenomenon Marie herself named "radioactivity." Working with crude equipment in a leaky, poorly ventilated shed, the couple processed tons of raw pitchblende by hand to isolate two entirely new elements, polonium and radium, forever changing our understanding of matter and the atom.

Tragedy struck in 1906 when Pierre was killed in a street accident, but Marie refused to let grief end her work. She took over his professorship, becoming the first woman ever to teach at the University of Paris, and pressed on with her research alone. Her achievements were recognized twice at the highest level: a Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903, shared with Pierre and Henri Becquerel, and a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911, awarded solely to her for the discovery and isolation of radium and polonium. To this day, she remains the only person ever to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields.

During the First World War, Curie turned her scientific knowledge toward saving lives, designing mobile X-ray units — nicknamed "Little Curies" — that she personally drove to the front lines so battlefield surgeons could locate bullets and shrapnel in wounded soldiers. She died in 1934 from aplastic anemia, almost certainly caused by decades of unprotected exposure to radiation, the very force she had spent her life studying. Her legacy endures not only in the elements and units of measurement that bear her family name, but in the countless scientists — especially women — who point to her as proof that rigorous, world-changing research has never been limited by gender or circumstance.

Key Milestones

1867Born in Warsaw

Maria Salomea Skłodowska is born in Russian-controlled Warsaw, the youngest of five children of two teachers.

1891Moves to Paris

She enrolls at the University of Paris (the Sorbonne) to study physics and mathematics, adopting the French name "Marie."

1895Marries Pierre Curie

She marries fellow physicist Pierre Curie, beginning a scientific partnership that would define both their careers.

1898Discovers Polonium and Radium

Working with Pierre, she identifies two new radioactive elements and coins the term "radioactivity."

1903First Nobel Prize

Shares the Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for research on radioactivity.

1906Takes Pierre's Chair at the Sorbonne

After Pierre's sudden death, she becomes the first woman to teach at the University of Paris.

1911Second Nobel Prize

Wins the Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone, for isolating pure radium and studying its properties.

1914–1918Mobile X-ray Units

Develops and drives mobile radiography vehicles to treat wounded soldiers during World War I.

1934Death

Dies of aplastic anemia in France, likely caused by prolonged radiation exposure from her research.
